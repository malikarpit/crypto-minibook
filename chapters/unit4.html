4.1 The Quantum Threat to Classical Cryptography

Classical computers work in binary (bits: 0 or 1). Quantum computers use qubits that can be in superposition (0 AND 1 simultaneously) and can be entangled (correlated at a distance). This allows quantum algorithms to explore exponentially many possibilities simultaneously.

Two Critical Quantum Algorithms

AlgorithmWhat It BreaksSpeedupImpact
Shor's Algorithm (1994) Integer Factorization & Discrete Logarithm Exponential → Polynomial time: O((log n)³) Breaks RSA, DH, ECC, DSA, ECDSA completely. ALL public-key crypto falls.
Grover's Algorithm (1996) Unstructured Search (symmetric crypto, hashes) O(2ⁿ) → O(2ⁿ⁄²) — square root speedup Halves effective key strength. AES-128 → 64-bit security. Fix: double key size. AES-256 remains safe.
⚠️
“Harvest Now, Decrypt Later” (HNDL): Nation-state adversaries are recording encrypted internet traffic today and storing it. When cryptographically-relevant quantum computers arrive (estimated 10–15 years), they will retroactively decrypt all stored traffic. Data with long secrecy requirements (medical, military, intelligence) is already at risk TODAY. This is why NIST standardized post-quantum algorithms in 2024.

⚡ Extra: How Many Qubits to Break RSA-2048?

Breaking RSA-2048 with Shor's algorithm requires approximately 4,000 logical qubits. However, due to quantum error rates, each logical qubit requires ~1,000–10,000 physical qubits for error correction. Current state-of-the-art (2025): IBM has ~1,000-qubit chips; Google demonstrated 105-qubit quantum supremacy on a specific task. A cryptographically-relevant quantum computer requires millions of physical qubits — still years away. But the “harvest now” threat makes migration urgent.

4.2 Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)

QKD — Quantum Key Distribution
Uses principles of quantum mechanics to distribute cryptographic keys. Any eavesdropping is physically detectable. Security is based on physics, not mathematical hardness — it is unconditionally secure (even against quantum computers).

Two Foundational Quantum Principles

  • No-Cloning Theorem: It is impossible to create an exact copy of an unknown quantum state. Eve cannot intercept and re-send a photon without introducing detectable errors.
  • Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle: Measuring a quantum state necessarily disturbs it. If Eve measures photons to spy, she irreversibly changes their polarization.

BB84 Protocol (Bennett & Brassard, 1984)

The first and most widely implemented QKD protocol. Uses single polarized photons over a fiber optic channel.

BB84 Step-by-Step
Bases: + (Rectilinear: 0°=0, 90°=1) or × (Diagonal: 45°=0, 135°=1) Step 1 - Alice transmits: Bit: 0 1 1 0 1 0 0 1 Basis: + × + × + + × × Step 2 - Bob measures (randomly choosing basis): Basis: + + + × × + + × Result:0 ? 1 0 ? 0 ? 1 (? = random when wrong basis) Step 3 - Sifting (over public channel): Bob announces which bases he used (NOT results) Alice says which bases were CORRECT (positions 1,3,4,6,8) Both keep only those bits: Raw Key = 0,1,0,0,1 Step 4 - Error Check: Compare a SUBSET of raw key bits publicly. If error rate > 11%: Eve is present, ABORT. If low: use privacy amplification to get final secure key.

E91 Protocol (Ekert, 1991)

Uses quantum entanglement instead of single photons. A central source emits entangled photon pairs — one to Alice, one to Bob. When both measure in the same basis, results are perfectly anti-correlated. Eavesdropping violates Bell's inequality, which Alice and Bob can detect statistically. More secure (detects all intercept-resend strategies) but harder to implement than BB84.

QKD Limitations

LimitationDetails
DistancePhotons are lost in fiber. Practical limit ~100–200 km without quantum repeaters (still experimental).
SpeedKey generation rate is slow — kilobits per second, not megabits.
CostRequires specialized hardware (single-photon sources, detectors). Expensive to deploy.
Implementation attacksThe QKD math is perfect; the hardware isn't. Detector blinding attacks exploit non-ideal single-photon detectors.
Authentication still neededQKD distributes keys but still needs an authenticated classical channel for the sifting step — which still requires PKI or a pre-shared key.
🔬 Advanced: Measurement-Device-Independent QKD (MDI-QKD)

Standard QKD is vulnerable to detector side-channel attacks (e.g., Eve blinds Alice's detector with a bright laser, then controls it). MDI-QKD (Lo, Curty, Qi, 2012) removes all detector side-channels by having both Alice and Bob send photons to an untrusted middle node (Charlie) that performs a Bell-state measurement. Charlie announces results publicly. Alice and Bob correlate their key bits based on Charlie's announcement — but Charlie learns nothing about the key. This makes QKD secure against all detector attacks, even if Charlie is completely compromised. Commercial MDI-QKD systems now exist (QuantumCTek, Toshiba).

Q1. In BB84, bases mismatch ~50% of the time. These bits are discarded. What is this step called?
Sifting (or basis reconciliation). Alice and Bob compare their chosen bases over a public (authenticated but not secret) channel. Bits where they chose different bases are discarded — only matching-basis bits form the sifted key. This keeps ~50% of transmitted bits.
Q2. Why can't a quantum eavesdropper copy photons to measure them later without disturbing the channel?
The No-Cloning Theorem: it is impossible to create an identical copy of an unknown quantum state. Eve cannot copy Alice's photons to measure at leisure — she must measure them now, disturbing them. The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle ensures this measurement changes the state, introducing ~25% errors in the sifted key that Alice and Bob detect.
  • BB84 protocol: 4 polarization states, 2 bases. ~50% bits sifted away after basis comparison
  • Security: No-Cloning Theorem + Heisenberg Uncertainty → eavesdropping always detectable (QBER > 11%)
  • QKD range: ~200 km fiber; Micius satellite extends to 1200 km via space
  • QKD is information-theoretically secure against quantum computers

4.3 Post-Quantum Cryptography (NIST PQC Standards)

Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)
Classical cryptographic algorithms designed to be secure against BOTH classical and quantum computers. They are based on mathematical problems that no known quantum algorithm can solve efficiently. No quantum hardware required — they run on today's computers.

NIST PQC Standards (Finalized August 2024)

FIPS StandardAlgorithm NameBased OnPurposeKey Sizes
FIPS 203 ML-KEM (CRYSTALS-Kyber) Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) over polynomial rings Key Encapsulation (replaces ECDH/RSA for key exchange) Kyber-512: 800B public key; Kyber-1024: 1568B
FIPS 204 ML-DSA (CRYSTALS-Dilithium) Module Lattices (MLWE + MSIS) Digital Signatures (replaces ECDSA/RSA-PSS) Dilithium3: 1952B public key, 3293B signature
FIPS 205 SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+) Stateless Hash-based signatures Digital Signatures (conservative backup, very different math from lattices) Larger signatures (~8KB) but based only on hash security
💡
Why Lattice-Based? The Learning With Errors (LWE) problem: given a system of approximate linear equations with noise, find the secret vector. No quantum algorithm can solve this significantly faster than classical algorithms. Lattice problems have been studied for 30+ years with no breakthroughs — unlike RSA and ECC which were broken by quantum algorithms.
⚡ Extra: How Kyber (ML-KEM) Works Conceptually

Kyber uses Module Learning With Errors (MLWE). Simplified analogy: Alice generates a secret vector s, computes a public matrix product A×s + e (where e is small random “noise”). Bob uses this public value to encrypt a message by adding more noise. Decryption works because Alice's secret s allows her to cancel out the systematic part, leaving only recoverable noise. A quantum computer cannot remove structured noise from lattice problems efficiently — Grover's algorithm provides only negligible speedup on random lattice problems.

🔬 Advanced: SIDH Break (2022) — A Warning

SIKE (Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation) was a NIST PQC finalist based on the hardness of computing isogenies between elliptic curves. In July 2022, Castryck and Decru published a classical (non-quantum) attack that broke SIKE in under an hour on a single laptop. This was a catastrophic failure — a promising post-quantum candidate eliminated overnight. The break exploited auxiliary information (torsion point images) that SIKE's key generation exposed. Lesson: PQC algorithms need even more public scrutiny than classical algorithms. NIST's multi-year competition was vindicated — the conservative lattice-based choices (Kyber, Dilithium) remain unbroken.

Q1. Shor's algorithm breaks RSA. What quantum resource does it actually require to threaten RSA-2048?
~4000 fault-tolerant logical qubits. Current quantum computers (2024) have ~1100 physical qubits with high error rates. Each logical qubit requires ~1000 physical qubits for error correction, meaning ~4 million physical qubits needed to break RSA-2048. Estimates suggest 10-15 years before this capability exists.
Q2. Why is "harvest now, decrypt later" an urgent threat even though quantum computers can't break RSA yet?
Nation-state adversaries record HTTPS/TLS traffic today. Once a quantum computer exists, they decrypt it retroactively using Shor's algorithm. Data with long sensitivity lifetimes (medical, state secrets, financial records) encrypted today remains exposed for decades. PQC migration must happen BEFORE quantum computers arrive — the timeline is 10-15 years, and TLS migrations have historically taken 10+ years.
  • Shor's: O((log N)³) — breaks RSA/ECC/DH; needs ~4M physical qubits for RSA-2048 (~15 years away)
  • Grover's: O(√N) search — halves symmetric key security; AES-256 remains secure (128-bit post-quantum)
  • NIST PQC 2022: Kyber (ML-KEM), Dilithium/Falcon/SPHINCS+ (signatures) — all lattice-based or hash-based
  • "Harvest now": recorded ciphertext decryptable later — PQC migration is urgent even before quantum computers exist

4.4 Homomorphic Encryption

Homomorphic Encryption
Allows arbitrary computations to be performed on encrypted data (ciphertext) without ever decrypting it. The cloud server processes ciphertexts and returns an encrypted result. Only the data owner with the private key can decrypt the final answer. The server learns nothing about the data.
Alice's Jewellery Box Analogy (from your resources)
Problem: Alice wants workers to assemble jewellery without touching raw materials. Solution: Alice puts materials in a LOCKED GLOVE BOX (only she has the key). Workers assemble through holes in the box. Alice unlocks to retrieve finished jewellery. Crypto equivalent: Encrypt = put data in locked box Homomorphic Evaluate = workers assemble (compute) inside locked box Decrypt = Alice unlocks and retrieves result The workers (cloud server) never see the raw materials (plaintext)!

Types of Homomorphic Encryption

TypeSupported OperationsDepthPractical Use
Partially Homomorphic (PHE) Either addition OR multiplication (not both) Unlimited for one operation RSA (multiplicative). Paillier (additive — used in e-voting, privacy-preserving statistics).
Somewhat Homomorphic (SHE) Both addition AND multiplication Limited — noise grows with operations Intermediate step. Can evaluate low-depth circuits. Used in some ML inference.
Fully Homomorphic (FHE) Any computation (Turing complete) Unlimited (via bootstrapping) Privacy-preserving ML, genomic analysis, secure cloud computing. Still slow.

The Noise Problem & Gentry's Bootstrapping

FHE ciphertexts contain cryptographic “noise” that grows with each operation. Addition adds noise linearly. Multiplication squares noise exponentially. Once noise exceeds a threshold, decryption fails.

Gentry's Bootstrapping (2009) — The FHE Breakthrough
Problem: After k multiplications, noise is too large to decrypt. Gentry's solution: Run the DECRYPTION CIRCUIT homomorphically! 1. Re-encrypt the noisy ciphertext under the PUBLIC KEY while it is still encrypted (encrypt-within-encrypt) 2. Homomorphically evaluate the decryption function on the doubly-encrypted ciphertext 3. Result: a FRESH ciphertext with minimum noise, same plaintext This "resets" the noise budget. Bootstrapping enables arbitrary-depth computation. Cost: One bootstrapping step takes seconds to minutes on current hardware.

Real Applications of FHE

  • Privacy-preserving ML inference: Send encrypted patient data to a hospital ML model; receive encrypted diagnosis. Hospital never sees raw patient data.
  • Genomic privacy: Research on encrypted DNA sequences without exposing personal genetic data.
  • Private database queries: Query a database without revealing what you searched for.
  • Secure e-voting: Tally votes homomorphically — sum encryptions without decrypting individual votes.
🔬 Advanced: CKKS Scheme — Approximate FHE for ML

The CKKS (Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song, 2017) scheme extends FHE to approximate arithmetic over complex numbers. Unlike BFV/BGV (exact integer arithmetic), CKKS allows small errors in the final result — acceptable for machine learning (a neural network with 0.001% error in weights is still accurate). CKKS encodes a vector of real numbers into a single ciphertext using SIMD packing, then performs polynomial arithmetic in a ring. A single CKKS ciphertext can encode thousands of float values, making batched neural network inference practical. Microsoft SEAL, IBM HELib, and Google's HEALt library all implement CKKS. Current performance: GPT-2 inference over encrypted data takes ~hours; still improving rapidly.

4.5 Blockchain & Cryptocurrency: Cryptographic Foundations

Blockchain
A decentralized, distributed, append-only ledger of transactions. Each “block” contains: transaction data, a timestamp, and the SHA-256 hash of the previous block header — creating a cryptographic chain. Altering any historical block invalidates all subsequent blocks.

Cryptographic Components of Blockchain

ComponentCrypto UsedPurpose
Transaction signingECDSA (secp256k1)Prove you own the funds you're spending. Your Bitcoin address IS your public key hash.
Transaction integritySHA-256Each transaction has a TXID = SHA-256(SHA-256(transaction data)).
Block linkingSHA-256Block header includes Previous Block Hash — linking them cryptographically.
Transaction groupingMerkle Trees (SHA-256)Efficiently prove a transaction is in a block without downloading the whole block.
Proof of WorkSHA-256 (mining)Find a nonce such that SHA-256(block_header) < target. Computationally expensive.

Merkle Tree Structure

A Merkle Tree is a binary tree of hashes. Leaf nodes = individual transaction hashes. Each parent = SHA-256(left child + right child). The root (Merkle Root) goes into the block header and represents all transactions.

Merkle Proof: Proving Tx-C is in a block (without downloading all 4 TXs)
Tree: Merkle Root / \ Hash(AB) Hash(CD) / \ / \ H(A) H(B) H(C) H(D) To prove H(C) is in the tree, you only need: - H(C) itself - H(D) (sibling) - Hash(AB) (uncle) Verify: Hash(Hash(H(C) + H(D)) + Hash(AB)) == Merkle Root? Only log2(N) hashes needed! For 1 million TXs: only 20 hashes.

Consensus Mechanisms

MechanismHow It WorksUsed ByEnergy
Proof of Work (PoW) Find nonce so SHA-256(block) < target difficulty. Whoever finds it first adds the block and earns the reward. Requires massive computation. Bitcoin ~150 TWh/year (comparable to Argentina)
Proof of Stake (PoS) Validators lock up ETH as stake. Algorithm pseudorandomly selects proposer weighted by stake. Penalties for dishonesty (slashing). Ethereum 2.0 ~99.95% less than PoW

Double-Spending Problem

Digital money can be copied (unlike physical cash). The double-spend problem: can the same digital coin be spent twice? Bitcoin solves this with the blockchain: every node has a full copy of all transactions. When I spend a coin, the network records it. If I try to spend it again, the network sees the UTXO (Unspent Transaction Output) is already consumed and rejects the second transaction. The 51% attack: if an attacker controls >51% of mining hash power, they can rewrite recent history — but this requires billions in hardware and is economically irrational (it would destroy the coin's value).

⚡ Extra: How Your Bitcoin Wallet Address Is Generated
1. Generate random 256-bit private key k 2. Compute public key K = k * G (ECC secp256k1 scalar multiplication) 3. Apply SHA-256 to K: SHA256(K) 4. Apply RIPEMD-160: RIPEMD160(SHA256(K)) = 20-byte Public Key Hash 5. Add version byte (0x00 for mainnet) 6. Apply double SHA-256 checksum, take first 4 bytes 7. Base58Check encode = your Bitcoin address (e.g., 1A1zP1...) Your address IS a double-hash of your public key. Your public key is only revealed when you SPEND (not receive).
🔬 Advanced: Quantum Threat to Blockchain

Shor's algorithm breaks ECDSA — meaning a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could forge any Bitcoin transaction. The threat is nuanced: (1) Your Bitcoin address hides your public key behind a hash (RIPEMD-160). Shor's can't directly break hashes (only Grover's applies). BUT (2) once you spend from an address, your public key is revealed on-chain. If a quantum computer exists, it can derive your private key from the revealed public key and steal remaining funds. Current estimate: ~4 million Bitcoin (~25% of supply) sits in addresses where the public key is already exposed. Post-quantum blockchain migration would require a coordinated hard fork to new CRYSTALS-Dilithium based signatures — a massive coordination challenge.

Q1. Schnorr ZKP: prover sends R=g^r, receives challenge e, sends s=r+e·x. Why does this prove knowledge of x without revealing x?
The verifier checks g^s = R · Y^e. This works because: g^s = g^(r+ex) = g^r · g^(ex) = R · (g^x)^e = R · Y^e. But s = r + e·x mixes x with the one-time random mask r. Without knowing r, the verifier cannot extract x from s — r is never revealed, and r is fresh for each proof.
Q2. What distinguishes zk-STARKs from zk-SNARKs?
STARKs: no trusted setup (use hash functions), quantum-resistant, larger proofs (~100 KB). SNARKs: require trusted setup (CRS/toxic waste), smaller proofs (~200 bytes), not quantum-resistant (use pairings). STARKs are preferred when trust minimization is critical (Ethereum StarkNet). SNARKs are preferred when proof size matters (Zcash, zkSync).
  • ZKP: prove knowledge of secret without revealing it. Three properties: Completeness, Soundness, Zero-Knowledge
  • Schnorr: 3-round interactive proof; foundation of EdDSA and many advanced protocols
  • zk-SNARKs: non-interactive, ~200-byte proofs, trusted setup required, pairing-based (not quantum-safe)
  • zk-STARKs: no trusted setup, quantum-resistant, ~100 KB proofs, hash-based
  • Applications: Zcash (private transactions), zkRollups (Ethereum L2 scaling), anonymous credentials

4.6 Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKP)

Zero-Knowledge Proof
A cryptographic protocol where a Prover can convince a Verifier that a statement is TRUE, without revealing any information beyond the truth of the statement itself. “I know the password, but I won't show you the password — I'll just prove I know it.”

Three Properties of ZKP

  • Completeness: If the statement is true and Prover is honest, Verifier will be convinced.
  • Soundness: If the statement is false, a cheating Prover cannot convince the Verifier (except with negligible probability).
  • Zero-Knowledge: The Verifier learns nothing beyond “the statement is true.” The proof reveals no additional information.
Ali Baba's Cave Analogy (classic ZKP illustration)
A cave has a ring-shaped tunnel with a magic door in the middle. Only the person knowing the password can open the door. Prover (Peggy) wants to prove to Verifier (Victor) she knows the password WITHOUT revealing it. Protocol (repeat 20 times): 1. Peggy enters the cave, randomly going LEFT or RIGHT (Victor doesn't see) 2. Victor shouts: "Come out from the LEFT" or "Come out from the RIGHT" 3. If Peggy knew the password: she can always exit the correct side. 4. If Peggy is cheating: she has only 50% chance of exiting correct side. After 20 rounds: probability of cheating = (1/2)^20 = 1 in 1 million. Victor is convinced Peggy knows the password, but learned nothing about it.
🔬 Advanced: zk-SNARKs (Succinct Non-Interactive Argument of Knowledge)

zk-SNARKs extend ZKPs to be non-interactive (no back-and-forth) and succinct (tiny proof sizes regardless of computation complexity). A zk-SNARK proves: “I know inputs w such that C(w) = y for a circuit C,” where the proof is only ~200 bytes and verifies in milliseconds, even if the circuit C has millions of gates. Used in:

  • Zcash: Proves transaction validity (outputs = inputs + fee) without revealing sender, receiver, or amount.
  • Ethereum rollups (zkSync, StarkNet): Prove 10,000 transactions are valid with one 200-byte proof, dramatically scaling Ethereum throughput.
  • Identity systems: Prove you are over 18 without revealing your birthdate.

zk-STARKs (Scalable Transparent Arguments of Knowledge) are a newer variant that require no trusted setup (eliminating a key weakness of zk-SNARKs) and are potentially post-quantum secure (based only on hash functions). Larger proof sizes (~50KB vs ~200B) but growing rapidly in adoption.

4.7 DNA Cryptography 🔬 Advanced Research

🔬 Advanced: Active Research Area (2024–2026)

DNA cryptography uses biological molecules (DNA strands) as a computational and storage medium for cryptographic operations. The four nucleotide bases (A, T, G, C) encode binary data: A/C = 0, G/T = 1 (or various encoding schemes).

Current Research Directions

Research AreaMethodSecurity Property
DNA-based OTP Key Distribution Synthetic DNA strands used as physical one-time pads. Nanopore sequencing generates identical random key streams at two distant locations. (CNRS, 2026) Unconditional security (OTP). Physical medium cannot be copied without altering the molecule. Resistant to quantum computers.
Unnatural Base Pairs (UBPs) Synthetic nucleotides (d5SICS, dNaM) not found in nature added to DNA. These cause termination errors in standard sequencers, providing biological obfuscation. Adds a layer of security: even if DNA is intercepted, standard sequencers cannot read the encoded data correctly.
STR Fingerprint Key Generation Short Tandem Repeat (STR) DNA fingerprints used as entropy source for generating encryption keys for IoT devices. Biometric-based keys — physically unclonable function (PUF) derived from unique genetic sequences.
DNA-based Secret Sharing Shamir's Secret Sharing encoded in DNA strands distributed among multiple participants. Each strand encodes a share; reconstruction requires combining physical molecules. Secret is physically distributed — no single point of compromise.
💡
Why DNA for Cryptography? (1) Massive density: 1 gram of DNA can store ~215 petabytes of data. (2) Physical uncloneability: the No-Cloning theorem applies — measuring quantum states of DNA molecules (like GC bonding patterns) disturbs them, providing inherent eavesdropping detection. (3) Longevity: DNA can store information for thousands of years (mammoth DNA recovered from permafrost). (4) It is immune to electromagnetic attacks.

Challenges & Limitations

  • High synthesis and sequencing costs (though falling rapidly)
  • Error rates in DNA synthesis and sequencing (~0.1–1% per base)
  • Slow read/write speeds compared to electronic storage
  • Temperature sensitivity — DNA degrades without proper storage
  • Not yet practical for real-time communication cryptography

🔬 Advanced: Cryptographic Agility — A Design Imperative

The SIKE break (2022) demonstrated that even well-vetted post-quantum candidates can fall. Cryptographic agility means designing systems with algorithm abstraction layers so that when an algorithm is broken, you swap it without rebuilding the entire system. TLS 1.3 already implements this via cipher suite negotiation. The US NSA's Commercial National Security Algorithm Suite 2.0 (CNSA 2.0) mandates cryptographic agility for all national security systems by 2030. For developers: never hard-code specific algorithms; use abstraction (e.g., Java JCA, Python cryptography library) that allows algorithm substitution.

4.9 Practice MCQs — Unit 4 (17 Questions)

Q1. Shor's algorithm can break RSA because it efficiently solves which mathematical problem?
(b) Shor's algorithm solves both Integer Factorization (breaking RSA) and Discrete Logarithm (breaking DH, DSA, ECC/ECDSA) in polynomial time. This makes all public-key cryptography based on these problems vulnerable to quantum computers.
Q2. In the BB84 protocol, how does Alice detect if Eve has intercepted the photons?
(b) Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: measuring a photon disturbs its state. When Eve measures and retransmits photons, she introduces ~25% errors into Bob's measurements. Alice and Bob compare a subset of their sifted key; if error rate exceeds ~11%, they abort.
Q3. Which NIST FIPS standard specifies the post-quantum key encapsulation algorithm?
(c) FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber). This is the post-quantum replacement for RSA and ECDH in key exchange. FIPS 204 is ML-DSA (Dilithium) for signatures. FIPS 205 is SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+) for hash-based signatures.
Q4. What is “Harvest Now, Decrypt Later” (HNDL)?
(b) Nation-state adversaries are recording all encrypted internet traffic now. When quantum computers mature in ~10-15 years, they will decrypt this stored traffic with Shor's algorithm. Data with long secrecy requirements (medical, military) is already at risk — making post-quantum migration urgent today.
Q5. Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) allows:
(b) FHE allows a cloud server to perform arbitrary computations on ciphertext without ever decrypting it. Only the data owner can decrypt the final result. This enables privacy-preserving cloud computing, ML inference, and genomic analysis.
Q6. Craig Gentry's bootstrapping technique solves which problem in FHE?
(b) FHE introduces cryptographic noise that grows with operations. Multiplication squares noise exponentially. Bootstrapping “resets” the noise by evaluating the decryption circuit homomorphically, producing a fresh ciphertext with minimum noise, enabling unlimited computation depth.
Q7. In a Bitcoin Merkle tree, the Merkle Root is stored in the:
(b) Block header. The block header (80 bytes) contains: version, previous block hash, Merkle root, timestamp, difficulty target, nonce. The Merkle root (32 bytes, SHA-256) cryptographically commits to all transactions in the block. Mining hashes the block header.
Q8. Zero-Knowledge Proofs allow a Prover to convince a Verifier of what, without revealing what?
(a) A ZKP proves the truth of a statement without revealing any information beyond the truth itself. The classic example: proving you know the solution to a puzzle (like a Sudoku) without showing the solution. “Soundness” ensures a cheater can't fake the proof.
Q9. Grover's quantum algorithm affects symmetric cryptography (AES) by:
(b) Grover's provides a square-root speedup for unstructured search. AES-128 (2¹²&sup8; classical) becomes 2&sup6;&sup4; effective strength (breakable). AES-256 becomes 2¹²&sup8; — still secure. Solution: use AES-256 instead of AES-128 in quantum-threat environments.
Q10. DNA cryptography provides which unique security advantage over mathematical cryptography?
(b) Like QKD, DNA strands exhibit quantum-mechanical properties. Measuring or copying a DNA key disturbs its state, providing inherent eavesdropping detection. Combined with OTP-style key generation from nanopore sequencing, DNA cryptography can be unconditionally secure — resistant even to quantum computers.
Q11. SIKE (Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation) was eliminated as a NIST PQC candidate because:
(b) In July 2022, Castryck and Decru published a classical attack exploiting SIKE's exposure of auxiliary torsion point images, breaking it in under an hour. This demonstrated that even well-vetted PQC algorithms can fail — reinforcing the need for algorithm diversity and cryptographic agility.
Q12. Bitcoin's Proof of Work consensus mechanism uses which hash function?
(c) SHA-256 (double SHA-256 = SHA-256(SHA-256(x))). Miners compute SHA-256(SHA-256(block_header)) repeatedly with different nonces until the result is below the current difficulty target. RIPEMD-160 is also used in Bitcoin but for address generation (RIPEMD-160(SHA-256(public_key))).
Q13. The E91 QKD protocol differs from BB84 in that it uses:
(b) E91 uses entangled photon pairs from a central source. When both Alice and Bob measure in the same basis, results are perfectly anti-correlated (entanglement). Eavesdropping breaks the entanglement, violating Bell's inequality measurably. BB84 uses single photons and error rate detection.
🍡 MediumQ14. NIST's 2022 PQC standardization selected which algorithm for key encapsulation (KEM)?
(b) CRYSTALS-Kyber (ML-KEM, FIPS 203). Kyber is based on Module-LWE and was selected for its balance of security, performance, and key size. CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA) and FALCON were selected for signatures. Rainbow (multivariate) was broken during the competition. McEliece has huge key sizes (~1 MB). NTRU is an alternate candidate but wasn't selected for primary FIPS standardization.
🔴 HardQ15. In BB84 QKD, if Eve intercepts and re-sends all photons, what error rate does she introduce in the sifted key?
(b) 25%. Eve randomly guesses the basis: 50% of the time she picks the right basis (no error), 50% she picks wrong (50% chance of error). Overall error rate = 0.5 × 0.5 = 25%. QBER (Quantum Bit Error Rate) threshold is ~11% for the standard BB84 protocol — anything above indicates eavesdropping and the key is discarded. In practice, channel noise also contributes to QBER.
🍡 MediumQ16. Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) allows computation on encrypted data. What is its primary current limitation?
(b) Performance overhead. FHE (Gentry, 2009) was a theoretical breakthrough but practical schemes (TFHE, CKKS, BGV) remain 1,000-10,000× slower than plaintext. A simple neural network inference that takes 1 ms in plaintext takes ~10 seconds with FHE. Active research (GPUs, FPGA acceleration) is reducing this. Current applications: private genomic analysis, encrypted database queries, privacy-preserving ML inference.
💀 ExpertQ17. In a zk-SNARK, what is the "trusted setup" and why is it a potential weakness?
(b) CRS with secret randomness. The CRS ("toxic waste") must be destroyed after setup. If retained, the holder can create proofs for FALSE statements — e.g., in Zcash, they could mint unlimited ZEC from nothing undetectably. Zcash's Powers of Tau ceremony (2018) involved 88 participants worldwide: as long as ONE participant destroyed their secret, the CRS is secure. zk-STARKs eliminate this with no trusted setup (using hash functions), but produce larger proofs (~100 KB vs ~200 bytes for SNARKs).
🍡 MediumQ14. NIST selected which algorithm as the primary post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism (KEM)?
(b) CRYSTALS-Kyber (ML-KEM). Selected in 2022 for balance of security, performance and key sizes. Kyber-768 provides ~180-bit security with 1184-byte public keys. Rainbow was broken during competition. McEliece has ~1 MB public keys. NTRU is an alternate but wasn't selected as the primary FIPS standard.
🔴 HardQ15. Grover's algorithm applied to AES-128 gives what effective post-quantum security level?
(b) 64-bit. Grover's algorithm searches an N-element database in O(√N) queries. For AES-128 (2^128 possible keys), Grover needs 2^64 quantum operations — equivalent to a 64-bit classical brute force. NIST recommends AES-256 for 128-bit post-quantum security (Grover on AES-256 → 2^128 quantum ops). AES-128 is therefore inadequate for long-term post-quantum security.
🍡 MediumQ16. What is "harvest now, decrypt later" and which existing algorithm is most threatened by it?
(b) Nation-state adversaries record encrypted communications today. RSA/ECDH key exchange is vulnerable to Shor's algorithm — once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists, all recorded sessions can be decrypted. Symmetric encryption (AES) is only weakly affected (Grover). The urgency: TLS migrations historically take 10+ years, and sensitive data lifetime may be 20+ years. PQC migration must start now.
💀 ExpertQ17. zk-SNARKs require a "trusted setup" generating a CRS. What is the catastrophic consequence if the CRS secret ("toxic waste") is not destroyed?
(b) Counterfeit proofs for false statements. In Zcash, valid SNARK proofs are required to create shielded ZEC. Toxic waste would allow minting unlimited ZEC from nothing, undetectably. Zcash's Powers of Tau ceremony used 88 participants — one honest participant destroys their secret, the full CRS is safe. zk-STARKs solve this with no trusted setup (hash-based), at the cost of larger proofs (~100 KB vs 200 bytes).
